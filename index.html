Check in by triage level

After the triage nurse assigns a level, one tap prints the ticket, texts the patient their link and puts them in the right place in line. Lower levels are seen first. The public screens show only the ticket number and level, never a name or the reason for the visit.

Why HALO exists

ER patients often sit for hours without knowing how long they’ll wait, why, or what to do if they start to feel worse. Our research found that the stress of waiting comes mostly from not knowing. A DePaul University study found that simply telling ER patients their wait time raised satisfaction by 18%, even when the wait wasn’t shorter.

Live wait list

Ticket numbers, triage levels and estimated waits update on their own. Tap a ticket to see why its wait changed.

Call for help

The button grows into an alarm, the tower’s ring flashes red, and a nurse is sent to you. You keep your place in line.

Map with directions

Tap a place for a route from “You are here,” with hours, walking time and a full map key.

Patient education video

A 30-second captioned spot: clean hands, ask before you eat or drink, and tell staff if you feel worse.

Scan or tap

Scan the QR code, or hold your phone over the tap pad like paying with your phone, to get the same page.

Private by design

Screens show ticket numbers and triage levels only, never names or reasons for the visit.

How we got here

We compared three concepts: a QR check-in ticket, the HALO kiosk, and a scan-from-your-seat live queue. The kiosk works for everyone, phone or not, so it became the core. From the other two we took the phone page and the nurse alert. Classmates rated the kiosk strongest but worried about cost, so the phone page lets one tower reach the whole room.

The tower

The 3D model on the Kiosk page is rebuilt from our PrusaSlicer print file: a 79 mm hexagonal tower on a stepped base, with a halo ring on top and a screen on each of its six sides.
